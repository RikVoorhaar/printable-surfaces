Writing contract

How this works

  1. One section at a time. No drafting a section before the previous one is approved. No exceptions, no "while I'm here" drafts.
  2. Two gates per section. Gate A: we agree, in rough bullets, what goes in the section. Gate B: the draft is written and reviewed rendered in the browser.
  3. Recall before grilling. When a section opens, the agreed bullets are restated back before any discussion of the draft, so the context is on the table.
  4. The draft is the only prose. It lives in blog/src/posts/3d_print_platonic_solids.mdx and is reviewed at http://100.81.11.110:4321/blog/3d_print_platonic_solids. This plan holds titles, sketches, bullets, status and open questions only.
  5. Nothing is recorded as agreed until it is agreed. A section's bullets box stays empty until we have actually talked it through.
  6. No length target. A section takes as many words as it takes. We cut things that aren't earning their place, not words to hit a number.
  7. Figures belong to the section that uses them. Chosen at gate A, produced before gate B, reviewed with the draft.

Facts to keep straight

Pulled from the code, because the story is easy to mis-remember.

FactTruth
Printability rule A rod is printable if its axis is at ≥ 48° elevation, or it is a horizontal bridge ≤ 10 mm; rods lying on the build plate are exempt. This is not the solid-overhang (“face normal”) rule.
Stretch Dodecahedron is the stretched one: face-down, ×1.93 vertical. Icosahedron is vertex-down, not uniformly stretched — its tip pyramids are lifted locally so the tip edges reach 50°.
No stretch Tetrahedron, cube and octahedron are regular, face-down, no stretch. (Vertex-down octa would have needed ×1.19 — that is why it is face-down.)
Pipeline Place (frame + constructive face fill + uniform interior) → evolve (force relaxation with support pull, 1/r² repulsion, anchor) → choose (MIP over candidate rods) → repair (degree 3, flat horizontals, upward links).
Coulomb gas It is a force term in the relaxation, not the sampling method. It gets a figure, not a section.
Where it hurts The horizontal top face: a ceiling of bridges with nothing below it. That is the real narrative turn, not “the icosahedron needs stretching”.
Still imperfect 14 cube / 4 octahedron nodes have no printable upward link (the layer under the ceiling is ~5.6 mm too low); top-face hatching has rods longer than the 10 mm bridge limit.

Sections

Each section below carries a short sketch of what I imagine is in it. These sketches exist so the whole article can be judged as a whole; they are not the agreed contents. When a section opens, gate A replaces the sketch with accepted bullets.

§1 Trying to print a mathematical surface, and hitting the overhang wall A · bullets pending B · draft pending

Sketch

The original ambition: take a mathematical surface, triangulate it, and print just the edges as a cage — sphere first, Klein bottle eventually. It fails for a boring reason: near the top and bottom of any such shape there are always rods too shallow to print, and no amount of clever triangulation fixes that on the skin alone.

The turn that sets up the rest: overhangs can be held from the inside, so the question becomes about the whole volume rather than the surface. Polytopes make that question checkable, because their faces are flat and their edges are few.

§2 When is a rod printable? Steep, short bridge, or on the plate A · bullets pending B · draft pending

Sketch

What the nozzle is actually doing: each layer is a bead laid on top of the previous one, so a cylinder rising steeply supports itself, while one lying nearly flat has nothing underneath. From that, the three ways a rod survives — steep enough, short enough to bridge, or resting on the plate — and where the numbers come from (48°, 5°, 10 mm).

The important reframing: this is a rule about the rod axis, not about the surface normal of the shape. That distinction is what makes the rest of the project possible, and it is also the part most write-ups get wrong.

§3 What the rule implies for a shape: convex polytopes and why the Platonic solids work A · bullets pending B · draft pending

Sketch

Apply the rule to a convex polytope sitting on one face. Its edges are either steep or horizontal; each face is either vertical, where steep rods fit, or horizontal, where only bridges fit. So the frame is printable almost for free, and the trouble is concentrated in exactly one place: a horizontal top face is a ceiling with nothing under it.

Stretching the shape vertically steepens the edges that would otherwise be too shallow, which is how the awkward solids are rescued — the dodecahedron at ×1.93, the icosahedron by lifting its tips rather than stretching it uniformly. This section ends by naming the ceiling as the thing the rest of the post has to solve, not the stretching trick.

§4 The interior is the hard part: placing nodes and relaxing them into printable positions A · bullets pending B · draft pending

Sketch

Nothing about the inside of the shape is automatically printable. The naive version — scatter points, connect the nearest ones, maybe push them apart with a Coulomb force so they spread evenly — produces something that looks right and prints badly, because "evenly spread" says nothing about having a parent below.

The fix is a two-part placement: a constructive bottom-up fill on the faces, which gives every face node parents by construction, and then a force relaxation over all nodes at once — a pull towards printable positions, a 1/r² repulsion to keep things even, and an anchor that drags interior nodes under the ceiling. After that, positions are good enough to choose rods from. This is where the Coulomb picture belongs, as one force among three.

§5 From points to a lattice: choosing rods under the printability rules A · bullets pending B · draft pending

Sketch

Positions are not a lattice yet. Enumerate candidate rods between nearby nodes, then pick a subset: reward printable rods and reasonable degree, forbid rods that collide, and insist that nodes which need a parent get one. That selection is a small integer program — described as "choose a good subset under constraints", with one figure and no solver talk.

The integer program cannot fix everything, so a repair pass follows: raise the minimum degree to 3, make every horizontal rod exactly horizontal, and give every node below the top a neighbour above it. Those repairs are allowed to break length and degree limits, but never the 48° rule — that ordering, and what it cost in failed attempts, is the honest core of this section.

§6 From lattice to object: thickness, clipping, and bed contact A · bullets pending B · draft pending

Sketch

A correct graph is not a printable part. The rods had to get thicker to survive handling, the joints thicker still so they can be trimmed without cutting the rods, and the clipping had to happen only at the faces — the first attempt cut the bottom rods in half and left hollow shells. The vertex-down icosahedron needed a small flat base, because a point cannot sit on a bed.

The print that exposed all of this: the first layers came out as isolated node cross-sections because joints dipped below the plate while rods did not. Ends on what it took to make the thing sit flat and solid — and what still doesn't.

§7 Checking the claim, what still fails, and the printed pieces A · bullets pending B · draft pending

Sketch

How the claim gets checked rather than asserted: classify every rod by its elevation, count the ones that fall in the dead band between a bridge and a steep rod, and see that the number is zero. Then the honest part — a handful of nodes with no printable upward link, and ceiling rods longer than the bridge limit, and why those are geometric rather than bugs.

The printed solids, the photo, and a short closing thought: the same rule should apply to other convex shapes and maybe to surfaces with enough interior, but the horizontal-face problem is still there, and that is a fine place to stop.
